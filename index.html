WEEK 02 — LEARNING NOTES

Building for the Web

Workshop Experience

Through website design and development, I began to connect theoretical knowledge with practical experience. Creating a website involves more than visual design. It also requires an understanding of page structure, content organisation and the technical relationships between different elements.

During the workshop, I used GitHub to work on my website. I encountered some difficulties with file formats and the way files were organised. Fortunately, Kaajal quickly came over and helped me identify and resolve the problem.

This week made me realise that users of digital media do not necessarily understand the technical structures behind the platforms they use. Through the process of creating a website, I gradually moved from being simply a consumer of online content to becoming a designer and creator of digital content.

I also began to think about how website design influences the way users understand information, and how the infrastructure of digital platforms can limit or shape user behaviour.

WEEK 03 — LEARNING NOTES

Web Scraping

Key Learning

This week focused on Web Scraping, particularly understanding the underlying structure of webpages and how to extract research-related data from online resources.

We learned that a webpage is not an indivisible whole. Instead, it is made up of HTML tags and different components. By identifying specific HTML elements, we can collect particular information and turn it into data that can be analysed further.

This helped me recognise that webpages are not only spaces for displaying information, but can also serve as data sources for researching digital society. Researchers can collect, compare and organise webpage data to examine how online content is structured and presented.

Preparation and Challenges

Before the workshop, I needed to understand HTML structures and the functions of different webpage elements. For someone without programming experience, the relationship between the code and the webpage we actually see can be difficult to understand at first.

In particular, identifying the data that needs to be extracted, distinguishing target information from surrounding irrelevant content, and understanding how HTML tags are used for filtering are areas I still need to improve.

Workshop Experience

During the workshop, I tried using a web scraping plugin to extract information from webpages. However, I still found it difficult to understand the hierarchical relationships between different webpage elements and determine exactly which level of the webpage I should extract data from.

My Reflection

This week encouraged me to look at the internet from a researcher's perspective. In the past, when browsing webpages, I mainly focused on the content displayed on the screen. Web scraping made me realise that the structure behind a webpage also has research value.

At the same time, data scraping does not mean that we can obtain complete or objective information. Researchers need to make decisions about what to collect, what to exclude and how to process the information. These choices can also influence the final research findings.

WEEK 04 — LEARNING NOTES

Data, Power, and Classification

Key Learning

This week explored the meaning of data in contemporary digital society and the relationship between classification and power.

I learned that data is not simply an objective record of the real world. The collection, organisation and classification of data all involve human choices. Different classification criteria may highlight certain information while overlooking other details. As a result, classification systems influence how we understand and interpret social phenomena.

In digital media environments, platforms use classification systems to organise information, identify users and manage content. These practices not only affect how information is presented, but may also influence different groups' opportunities to access resources and participate in digital society.

Preparation and Challenges

Before the workshop, I began to question whether data can ever be completely objective and how classification criteria are established.

One question I found particularly interesting was whether important social contexts and individual differences might be lost when researchers transform complex social phenomena into a limited number of data categories.

Workshop Experience

During this week's workshop, our group collected and classified opinions about university students' and educators' attitudes towards AI. We gathered posts from several platforms, including Twitter, Xiaohongshu, Bilibili and Facebook.

I was responsible for collecting 30 posts from Twitter. I found that approximately 70% of the students and educators represented in the posts supported the reasonable use of AI. This was somewhat surprising to me.

My Reflection

This week encouraged me to think more deeply about the objectivity of data.

I might previously have assumed that data could provide relatively objective facts. However, I am now beginning to realise that the meaning of data depends on how it is collected, selected and classified.

In digital media research, classification is not simply a way of organising information. It is also a practice with social implications. It may determine which information becomes visible, which groups are recognised and which experiences are more likely to be overlooked.

WEEK 05 — LEARNING NOTES

Data Visualisation

Key Learning

This week focused on Data Visualisation and how complex datasets can be transformed into visual forms that are easier to understand and communicate.

We discussed how data visualisation is not only a tool for presenting research findings, but can also be a research method in itself. By observing relationships, distributions and patterns in data, researchers may discover things that are not immediately obvious and develop further research questions.

We also explored the relationship between data visualisation and storytelling. Different chart types, visual designs and ways of organising information can all influence how audiences interpret data.

Audience Awareness

The workshop highlighted three important questions to consider before creating a visualisation:

  • Who is the audience?
  • What do you want your audience to think, feel, or do?
  • What key messages should the audience receive?

Preparation and Challenges

Before the workshop, I considered the differences between data visualisation and traditional ways of presenting data.

One important challenge is how to simplify complex datasets while still preserving necessary information and detail. Excessive simplification may cause important social contexts to be overlooked, while overly complex visualisations can make information difficult for audiences to understand.

Workshop Experience

During the workshop, we used data collected in the previous week, alongside other publicly available datasets, to conduct analysis and create visualisations.

We used Tableau Public as our visualisation tool. One of our tasks involved analysing posts from 32 friends. Since I do not have many friends on Instagram, I decided to collect data from Xiaohongshu instead.

I found that my friends' posts and interests were mainly related to cute pets and fashion. There was also a high degree of similarity between their content. Perhaps this is one of the reasons why we became friends in the first place. :)

My Reflection

This week made me realise that data visualisation is not a completely neutral process of communicating information.

Even when using the same dataset, different chart types, colours, proportions and selection methods can create different narratives. Therefore, researchers need not only to ensure data accuracy, but also to remain critically aware of the effects of visual design.

I believe that effective data visualisation should not only help audiences understand data quickly, but also encourage them to consider the social context behind it, rather than simply using visual effects to persuade audiences to accept a particular conclusion.

WEEK 06 — LEARNING NOTES

Identity, Algorithmic Identity, and Data

Key Learning

This week explored how algorithms collect and analyse user data to identify and classify individuals, and how algorithmic identity is formed within digital platforms.

The course referred to Gillespie's (2014) definition of algorithms, describing them as encoded procedures that transform input data into expected outputs. Algorithms also filter information considered relevant based on data generated through users' behaviours, preferences and online expressions.

We also discussed Cheney-Lippold's concept of algorithmic identity. Rather than being fixed, algorithmic identity is dynamic and is constructed through the data users continuously generate, including their online behaviours, social relationships, consumption habits and activities on digital platforms.

This helped me understand that algorithms do not simply identify users. They can also influence what users see, what information they encounter and how they participate in digital platforms.

Preparation and Challenges

Before the workshop, I needed to reconsider the relationship between algorithms, data classification and identity.

In particular, I wanted to understand the difference between algorithmic identity and personal identity in real life, as well as why an algorithm's interpretation of a user may change over time and in response to different behaviours.

Workshop Experience

This week's workshop focused on three main stages:

  • Input: Thinking about what personal information we share online and what kinds of data platforms can collect about us.
  • Output: Examining advertising profiles generated by different platforms to understand how they classify individual users.
  • Process: Exploring how algorithms work and how user data is transformed into judgements and predictions about individuals.

Through this process, we moved beyond simply using digital platforms and began to observe how platforms collect, process and interpret personal data.

I examined my own content preference profile on Xiaohongshu. The main categories included celebrity information, makeup and food-related content. However, I did not fully agree with the platform's classification of celebrity information as my largest content category, because I do not follow any particular celebrity very closely.

My Reflection

This week gave me a more personal understanding of digital identity.

I began to realise that the version of me understood by a platform is not necessarily the same as who I am in real life. Algorithms can only construct user profiles based on the data that platforms are able to collect, and this data usually represents only one part of a person's life.

For example, occasionally searching for a particular product does not necessarily mean that someone is consistently interested in it. Similarly, browsing a certain type of content does not always reflect a genuine personal preference.

Therefore, algorithmic identity reflects users' activities in digital spaces, but it is also limited by the ways platforms collect and classify data.

This also led me to think further about power relations within digital platforms. When algorithms continuously predict and classify users based on existing data, do they also influence the information users encounter and the choices they make in the future?

My most important takeaway this week was a shift in perspective: instead of simply using algorithms, I have started to question how they understand, define and influence the people who use them.

03 — REFLECTIONS

Thinking beyond the screen.

Across these weeks, I have gradually begun to look beyond the visible interfaces of digital platforms. From website structures and web scraping to data classification, visualisation and algorithmic identity, I have become more aware of the choices and processes behind everyday digital experiences.

Rather than treating technology as neutral or independent, I am learning to question how digital systems are designed, how information is organised and whose perspectives are represented or overlooked.

This journal will continue to develop as I explore the relationship between digital technologies, society and everyday life.
